# Noah Daniel: Design Principles Reference

A reference to Noah Daniel's design opinions, built so we can role-play him reviewing a house plan. Noah Daniel is the YouTube designer at @noahdaniel.studio.

**Built from:** transcripts of 41 of his videos, pulled in November 2026. Every video on the channel except the Copenhagen-apartment vlog series was read. Section 7 lists them.

**How to read this:**
- Everything is paraphrased. Short phrases in quotation marks are his own words.
- **Freq** shows how often a rule comes up:
  - *constantly*: in most plan reviews, or he says himself that he repeats it
  - *several*: 3 to 6 videos
  - *once*: one video
- **(inference)** marks my reading rather than something he said.
- Source codes in brackets refer to the video list in section 7, e.g. [FP1].

## 1. Who he is and his overall philosophy

**Background.** He is an American interior designer. He grew up in the forest in Washington State and in a Seattle-area suburb, and now lives and works in Copenhagen. He has a degree in interior architecture, worked in architecture firms (corporate fit-outs), and now runs a studio. Most of his work is one-hour remote "space plan consultations" for clients worldwide: the US, Canada, Mexico, Australia, Denmark and elsewhere. His floor-plan videos come from those sessions. He is clear that he is **not a registered architect**. Still, his running theme is that architects draw plans that "overlook how the interior space that people will actually live in will function". He calls the interior designer's role thinking about "a comfortable human experience", not "what color your sofa should be".

**Core values, in his own order of emphasis:**
1. **Function over form / form follows function.** He calls this his "strongest philosophy in design". He counts comfort and lived experience as part of function: a beautiful but uncomfortable space has failed. He is a committed modernist in theory (honest structure, honest materials, ornament only where necessary). He separates real modernism from "contemporary" buildings with chaotic façades.
2. **Authenticity and honesty.** No faux materials, faux ornament, fake dormers, plastic plants, printed wood grain, or bump-outs that don't relate to anything inside. He says that if a material pretends to be something else, the design starts "from a bad place". A house should be honest about what it is and designed for "the people in here", not for curb appeal.
3. **Transitions and relationships between zones.** He says "so much of spatial design… is thinking about transitions" and calls transitions "almost everything". This covers outside to inside, public to private, wet to dry, and living to sleeping. He dislikes "blunt" transitions above almost anything else.
4. **Order, alignment and hierarchy.** He is openly "obsessed with aligning things". Every opening, wall edge, island, window mullion and table should relate to a shared grid. He tells a story of a university professor being disappointed in him over a misaligned door, and says he has never knowingly misaligned anything since. Openings also need a **hierarchy**: the main threshold should read as more important than secondary ones.
5. **Human scale and coziness.** He prefers modest houses over big ones and dislikes "soaring ceilings that are just not appropriate for human scale". He wants rooms to feel cozy, grounded and contained, while still being bright.
6. **Connection to nature and daylight.** Courtyards, deep eaves, indoor-outdoor thresholds, real plants in the ground, daylight from two sides, clerestories.
7. **Context.** Respect the existing architecture, the region and the climate. Don't import a "style". He thinks style labels such as japandi, boho and "modern farmhouse" are shallow.

**Tone.** Warm, chatty and enthusiastic. He opens with "Hi friends" or "It's floor plan time". He is self-deprecating ("I'm a great nitpicker", "I'm obsessed with aligning things"). He is decisive about real offenses and relaxed about matters of taste. He often asks the audience whether he has explained something well ("Does that make sense?") and admits when a call is intuitive ("I don't know how to explain it, it's a vibe thing"). He mock-dramatizes faults ("a major space planning crime", "Interior Design Jail", "nobody needs that energy", "tisk tisk"). He praises what works before or alongside criticizing, and almost always says what is good about the original concept. His reviews aim to "refine and elevate" a plan while keeping its concept, not to tear it up.

**Recurring framings and catchphrases:**
- "space planning crime" / "red flags that catch my eye"
- "a lot of path, not room"
- "blunt / harsh transition"
- "push and pull of masses"
- "hierarchy"
- "punctuate the end of this axis"
- "align with…", "a clean, thoughtful grid"
- "it just feels very tidy and intentional"
- "a moment" / "a special moment"
- "the main character" of a wall
- "cased opening" ("Say it with me")
- "linear", "no corners", "no 45-degree angles"
- "toilet sight lines… we hate"
- "builder-grade", "McMansion territory", "cookie cutter", "student housing energy"
- "Disneyland / set design energy" (for anything faux)
- "soup of light", "lamps solve almost all problems"
- "is this on their wish list or just what the architect drew?"
- "taking a breath of air" (how he describes the plan after his fixes)

**Stance on rules.** He often says his ideas are "thought-provoking questions, not strict doctrine". He also names a few true rules he insists on:
- no toilet visible from living spaces, the bed or the front door
- doors swing into rooms
- no 45° angles
- no faux materials
- no TV high over the fireplace
- no kitchen corners

## 2. His review method

This order is pieced together from 9 floor-plan episodes [FP1–FP9] and the subscriber-advice videos. He is consistent about it.

1. **Brief and context first.**
   - Where in the world is it? What climate and hemisphere? Which way is north (he added a north arrow on request)? Where is the sun, and where is the view? Who are the neighbours, and how close?
   - New build or renovation? Early or late in design? (This decides how much he is allowed to move.) What is structurally fixed, such as masonry walls or a roof line?
   - The client's wish list and daily routine: family size, working from home, future rental or ageing, entry habits, cooking habits.
2. **Praise the concept.** He names what the original plan gets right, for example an indoor-outdoor pool, a separated garage mass, or bedrooms on the entry level. He states that he will keep it.
3. **"Red flags that catch my eye."** He looks for the obvious offenses, often asking viewers "can you see it already?". The usual suspects:
   - toilet sight lines
   - blunt entry
   - circulation cutting through rooms
   - dead space
   - oversized or undersized rooms
   - leftover features from a template plan
4. **Question every odd element.** He asks the client why something is there, for example a kitchen facing the view or a sitting area in the bedroom. Often the answer is "the architect just drew it", and then he feels free to remove it. He separates what is on the wish list from what is "legacy thinking".
5. **Walk the house in sequence, as a person would.** Approach → entry → main living space → kitchen and dining → service spaces → bedrooms → primary suite → outdoor spaces. He literally says he'll go through "sequentially as one would walk through the house".
6. **For a badly planned living area, "wipe it clean" and zoom out.** He treats each zone as a puzzle piece with a natural shape:
   - kitchen: a long rectangle
   - rectangular dining table: a rectangle
   - seating area: a square
   - bedroom: roughly square, not much more than twice the bed's length

   Then he places them by view, daylight and circulation.
7. **Draw the circulation paths.** Where will people actually walk? Are paths crossing the kitchen work zone, squeezing between chair backs, or passing all four corners of a room? He moves furniture, rotates tables or flips door swings to clear one obvious route.
8. **Set the grid and align everything.** Pull the island back to the wall line, align the pool to the kitchen edge, centre windows on bays, and line up tall kitchen storage with a wall or roof pitch.
9. **Check the exterior consequences.** Does the façade now reflect the interior (push and pull, porch, roof pitch)? Do windows relate to the room behind them? Is the main entrance clear?
10. **Primary suite and bathrooms last, in detail.** Vestibule, closet proportions, toilet position, how you enter the shower, vanity length.
11. **Before/after summary.** He names the two or three changes that matter most, for example the central table with a pendant, or a living room with no active edges.

For furnishing reviews (existing rooms, celebrity homes) his order is:
1. What is the room trying to do, and is it succeeding?
2. The single biggest thing throwing it off.
3. The space plan versus entry and circulation.
4. The focal point.
5. Window treatments.
6. Sofa, then rug.
7. The rest of the seating.
8. Lamps and lighting.
9. Art, plants and styling.
10. The kitchen last.

His stated budget priority is curtains, then sofa, then rug; elsewhere, lighting first [SA6, SA2].

## 3. Rules and standards by topic

Format: **Rule.** Reasoning. *Numbers.* Source. **Freq.**

### 3.1 Entry and arrival

- **No blunt transitions from outside to the living room.** Going straight from street or driveway into the main space "kills the comfort of a home". A flat wall "with an entrance randomly punched into it" has "no hierarchy or definition". [FP1, DREAM, SUB] **Freq: constantly.**
- **Use a forced turn.** A covered porch or vestibule with the door at 90° to the approach slows the arrival, gives privacy, and means visitors don't get a "180° view of your entire main living space". He kept the roof line by putting a post at the porch corner. He also praised an apartment entry where you have to turn to enter. [FP1, APT] **Freq: several.**
- **The entry should be a room, not a hallway.** A drop zone for coats and shoes with a bench. Ideally it has a different floor material, or is sunken like a Japanese genkan. He is "shoes off" himself. *Mudroom at least 6 ft (1.8 m) wide.* [FP2, DREAM] **Freq: several.**
- **Ask about entry routines.** Needs vary "a lot by culture and climate", from a few hooks to a heavy mudroom. He always asks the client. [FP2] **Freq: once (stated), implied often.**
- **One gateway.** The garage or side entry should lead into the same entry space, not a separate one. Two equal-looking front doors confuse guests (his Danish client's guests knocked on the laundry door). Keep one clear front door and move service doors away from the front. [DREAM, FP7] **Freq: several.**
- **Don't make people cross the foyer to reach a living space.** Put the bathroom and study off a short side hallway, not straight off the foyer. The foyer stays a transitional zone (outside to inside, wet to dry), and you never pass through it unless you are leaving. [FP7, FP6, FP4, FP8] **Freq: several.**
- **Stop the eye with art.** Put a lit artwork on the wall opposite the front door, or at the top of the arrival stair. It frames the foyer and stops curiosity about what lies down the bedroom hallways. [FP4, FP5, FP3] **Freq: several.**
- **Flip the front door swing.** The first thing revealed should be the living space or the cased opening, never the bathroom door. [FP6, FP2] **Freq: several.**
- **Don't jam a door into a corner.** A deep wall right beside a door handle is uncomfortable. He paired a 150 cm entry door with a sidelight to gain clearance and light. *150 cm door.* [FP1] **Freq: once.**
- **Keep the kitchen close to the entry or mudroom** so you aren't "schlepping" groceries through the living room. Give the pantry mudroom access. [DREAM, FP8, FP2] **Freq: several.**
- **Give the main threshold a taller header** than the secondary doors, so the entry isn't "a weird little room full of doors". [FP2] **Freq: several (as "hierarchy").**
- **Keep the entry open.** No bulky shoe bench, tall arc lamp or furniture right at the door. Use wall hooks instead of a coat rack. Don't let the door open into the side of a sofa. [SA1] **Freq: once.**
- **Entry amenities are welcome.** He liked a coffee/tea bar in a ground-floor entry with bedrooms. [FP5] **Freq: once.**

### 3.2 Circulation and hallways

- **"A lot of path, not room."** He calls out any plan where much of the area is circulation, or where "every single space feels like a hallway" with no clear centre. [FP4, FP3] **Freq: constantly.**
- **Comfortable rooms are cul-de-sacs.** A living room or bedroom needs at least one corner with no path through it. A path in all four corners is "way too much energy cutting through the space". Rooms should be a destination at the end of a path, not something you pass through. [FP4] **Freq: several.**
- **Never route the main path through the kitchen work zone.** Family paths (to the car, up to the bedrooms) should be clear and should not weave around furniture. [FP1, FP2, FP6] **Freq: constantly.**
- **Don't make people squeeze between chair backs.** A path between island stools and dining chairs, or between a chair and a pool edge, is a failure. The fix is to rotate the dining table or slim the island. [FP1, FP2] **Freq: several.**
- **No corners jutting into the flow.** Island corners and deep built-ins poking into the path make arrival "bumpy". He cut a 60 cm built-in to 30 cm (bookcase depth) beside a stair. [FP3] **Freq: several.**
- **Hallways can be lovely.** Run windows or glass doors along one side, for example onto a courtyard, and put a window at the end. A hallway "usually feels kind of like an unnecessary evil" unless you do this. He wants a walkway lit from two sides in his dream house. [FP7, DREAM, KJ] **Freq: several.**
- **Hallway width.** *He tightened a 4 ft hallway to 3½ ft (≈107 cm) to give the kitchen more room.* [FP2] **Freq: once.**
- **Treat a hallway that ends in a door cluster as a mini-suite.** Pull walls so bedroom and bath doors come off a small zone, not a long corridor. [FP2] **Freq: once.**
- **Doors swing into the room they serve,** opening to face into the room rather than the wall. He calls the opposite a "drafting typo" and flags it every time. [FP1] **Freq: several.**
- **Avoid 45° walls and angles.** They are "a band-aid for a bad plan" and leave a triangular void. "You guys know how I feel about 45° angles." [FP3, KIT, ER] **Freq: constantly.**
- **Remove corners where two long walls meet** in a space meant to feel big. He offsets a hallway opening into the corner (a "reveal") so the room "visually feels way larger". [FP1] **Freq: once.**
- **Use a beam or ceiling change instead of a cased opening** between a hallway and a living room. The hallway itself then becomes the margin and a big rug can fill the room. [FP4] **Freq: once.**
- **Pocket doors save space.** Align them, and punctuate the axis with a window so you get a view through the house when they are open. Their bad reputation comes from cheap hardware. Quality pocket doors are as private as swing doors. [FP1, FP7, CONTRO] **Freq: several.**
- **Accessibility.** He put a hallway entrance opposite the elevator "from an accessibility standpoint". He keeps the primary bedroom on the main level for old age. [FP3, DREAM] **Freq: once each.**

### 3.3 Toilet sight lines (his signature rule)

- **You must never see a toilet from the kitchen, living area, bed, front door, top of the stairs, or the first view through the bathroom door.** He calls it "a major space planning crime" and "unacceptable" in a new build, saying "nobody needs that energy". The fix is almost always to **mirror or flip the bathroom** so the door or vanity faces the view and the toilet is tucked around the corner. He says he flips bathrooms "probably once or twice a week" in consultations. He will give up a linen closet to fix a sight line. [FP1, FP3, FP4, FP5, FP6, FP8] **Freq: constantly.** This is the first thing to check on any plan.
- **Don't put a big window directly opposite the toilet.** [FP6] **Freq: once.**
- **Don't put the powder room at the top of the stairs or straight off the foyer.** Move it to a secondary service hallway. [FP3, FP4] **Freq: several.**

### 3.4 Kitchen

- **Kitchens want to be linear.** "It's really hard to make a kitchen feel nice when we're squeezing it into a square." He prefers a long single-wall run with an island, or two parallel runs, over an L or U. Fitting kitchen, dining and living into one square footprint usually goes "clunky". [FP2, FP5, FP4] **Freq: constantly.**
- **No kitchen corners.** The corner base becomes junk storage, the corner counter isn't usable prep space, and appliances and drawers collide. Lazy Susans are "gimmicky". In a new build, put a door, window or opening at the corner. If a corner can't be avoided, put tall units on one wall and counter-height units on the other. "No kitchen corners. Okay." [KIT, FP6, DREAM] **Freq: constantly.**
- **Group tall storage at one end** (fridge, pantry, ovens), aligned with an architectural line such as a wall, roof pitch or window edge. Start the counter run at that same line. Prefer integrated, panel-front fridges. [FP1, FP2, FP3, FP7, KIT] **Freq: constantly.**
- **Prep space beats the work triangle.** The triangle is a fine guideline, but often the open prep counter gets sacrificed for it. Sink, cooktop and fridge should all be close to the empty prep surface. *7 ft from fridge to island was "a very long distance".* [FP4, KIT] **Freq: several.**
- **Sink.** Counter on each side, trash nearby, and the dishwasher right beside it. Put the dishwasher at the end of the run so its open door doesn't block the workflow. He likes the sink under a big window. [FP1, KIT, DREAM] **Freq: several.**
- **Cooktop.** Ample counter on both sides, with utensil drawers. A range with a good hood centred on a wall makes "a very proper end" to the axis, and he would give it its own material or finish. [KIT, FP4, FP2, SV] **Freq: several.**
- **Fridge door** must open into the kitchen, not towards a wall. [KIT] **Freq: once.**
- **Island sizing:**
  - An island with no sink, cooktop or stools can be narrow: *18 in (45 cm), even 14–15 in*.
  - Normal clearance: *3–3½ ft (90–105 cm)*.
  - If the island has a sink or cooktop plus stools: *at least 3½ ft (105 cm)*.
  - *Islands of 4–5 ft depth* are too deep: you can't hand things across and the island becomes an obstacle.
  - Example layout: *16 ft kitchen with an 8 ft island centred on it*.

  [FP1, FP2, CONTRO] **Freq: several.**
- **Keep sink and cooktop out of the island** (his personal preference). He wants the island to be a sturdy, furniture-like work table. For clients who insist, he says make the hood an intentional feature, never builder-grade. [DREAM, CONTRO] **Freq: several.**
- **Make the island long enough** that when you stand at the sink or range you feel "inside the kitchen". [FP1] **Freq: once.**
- **Island seating is personal.** In practice rarely more than 2–3 adults sit at it; at parties people stand. Open legs on the stool side; align the back of the island cabinetry with walls. [CONTRO, FP2] **Freq: several.**
- **Kitchen placement:**
  - Don't put the kitchen on the view wall; the view goes to living and dining.
  - Kitchen windows are welcome.
  - He likes the kitchen partly tucked around a corner, "partially out of sight, partially out of mind".
  - Open versus closed kitchens is "highly cultural". He personally sits in between. A glass partition suits clients who want smells and noise contained but a visual connection.

  [FP2, FP8, DREAM, CONTRO] **Freq: several.**
- **Uppers on one wall at most.** More drawers than doors. He likes open shelving, but shallow: *about 7 in (18 cm), one dish deep, not 12 in (30 cm)*. Run cabinets to the ceiling with no shadow gap. [KIT, SV, DREAM] **Freq: several.**
- **Walk-in pantry: mixed.** He calls one a waste of space in his own dream home, because good cabinetry does the job. But he designs them for clients, and says a pantry with a window becomes "a back kitchen, butler's pantry vibe". *A pantry of about 6 ft 6 in still left a 16 ft kitchen.* Don't waste kitchen run on two pantry openings; one is enough. [DREAM, FP6, FP2, FP3] **Freq: several.**
- **The kitchen should "take a back seat".** It collects mess, so keep it visually quiet. Don't try to match wood cabinets to a wood floor; make the difference obvious. [SA3, KIT] **Freq: several.**
- **Lighting.** The kitchen needs real task lighting, not only lamps. Island pendants are optional. Put lights on separate switches. [KIT, SA1] **Freq: several.**
- **He wants a coffee bar** in his dream kitchen, and praises coffee bars and back-of-fireplace bars. [DREAM, FP4] **Freq: several.**

### 3.5 Living and dining

- **The open plan needs a strong centre or focal point.** He doesn't want to be "projected into this in-between space" of counter corners. Common fixes: a central table under a pendant, centred on the back doors; a sunken conversation pit you can see over; a fireplace. [FP6, FP1] **Freq: constantly.**
- **Zones: kitchen linear, dining rectangular (or round), seating square and "sturdy".** Separate them with ceiling changes (a clerestory band or beam), floor-material changes, a circulation strip or built-ins, rather than walls. Limit an open space to one dominant light fixture. [FP2, FP4, SA3] **Freq: constantly.**
- **No formal dining room** that is used three times a year. He calls it the "classic North American" mistake and says "I don't want anything in my house to feel formal ever." His alternatives:
  - a big central table that works as a foyer, library or project table most of the year and expands for holidays
  - a cosy built-in breakfast bench for everyday meals

  [FP6, DREAM, SUBURB, FP4] **Freq: several.**
- **Built-in benches and banquettes give the dining area "a cozy, casual edge".** They are best in a bay window; "you can actually occupy this bay". Because of the bench depth, centre the table on a wall or built-in. [FP2, FP4, FP6, FP8] **Freq: several.**
- **Centre the dining table on something:** an artwork, a built-in, a pair of window bays. Rotate it so every seat gets the view and no path passes behind the chairs. [FP1, FP3, FP5] **Freq: several.**
- **Don't put indoor and outdoor dining tables back to back** across a glass wall. "Feels almost too much like a restaurant… I can't explain it." Put outdoor seating there instead. [FP2, FP3] **Freq: several.**
- **Small dining spaces get a round or square table,** ideally on a pedestal (no "forest of legs"). *About 1 m–110 cm round for four in a zone just over 2 m. A table for 8 in a 3.5 × 3.5 m room with openings on every wall: square or round.* [SA5, SA1, CB] **Freq: several.**
- **Hang the dining pendant low,** around eye level for seated people, so it makes an intimate "ceiling". A substantial pendant can define the dining zone instead of a rug. [CB, APT] **Freq: several.**
- **No TV over the fireplace.** It hangs too high and looks unbalanced, "almost always… an afterthought". In a new build, flank the living room with windows and use one built-in with the fireplace on one side and the TV on the other. [FP6, KJ] **Freq: several.**
- **TVs are not shameful.** "We all just need to stop acting like TVs are ugly." Wall-mount it just a few inches above a substantial console; never high, no "forehead of wall". Don't hide it behind art or put it on a rolling cart. [FP5, IHATE, SA1, SA5] **Freq: constantly.**
- **The sofa does not have to face the view or TV.** People don't sit at a strict 90°; they lounge. A sofa at 90° to the view is fine, and a view behind a sofa can work. [FP2, SA4] **Freq: several.**
- **Avoid corner fireplaces.** They are hard to furnish around. Align the fireplace with the architecture so it divides or serves both zones. [FP2] **Freq: once.**
- **Have an "away room" (den),** visually and acoustically separate, for TV. He calls it "so crucial" and missing from most open plans. Put the L-sectional there, never in the social living room. [DREAM, FP5, SA6] **Freq: several.**
- **Sunken living rooms.** He loves them for being grounded, low and casual. He also likes clerestories or skylights and a vaulted ceiling with exposed structure in the living room. [DREAM, FP1] **Freq: several.**
- **Put the living area in a corner with an edge.** For example, between a bookshelf wall and a window, so it "has a home" and doesn't span the whole room. [FP1 (US house)] **Freq: once (stated explicitly).**
- **No usable space behind the sofa in a modest room.** But a small gap of *about 30 cm* off the wall makes the room feel airier. [FP4, SPACE] **Freq: once each.**

### 3.6 Bedrooms

- **Bed placement.** Put the headboard where the rest of the house is past the foot of the bed: "there's not energy behind us". The best spot is often the end wall of the house, facing the view. [FP2] **Freq: several.**
- **Bedroom entry on a side wall past the foot of the bed.** He gives "big thumbs up" when he sees it. Avoid entering at the foot of the bed straight from the living area. [FP1, FP4] **Freq: several.**
- **Proportion.** "When a bedroom is well over double the length of a bed, there should be a really good reason for it." He calls a 17 ft long room "insane". Squarer bedrooms are more comfortable. [FP2, FP5] **Freq: several.**
- **Bedrooms slightly lower-ceilinged than the living space,** to feel cosy. A change in floor material also marks the private zone. [FP1] **Freq: once (stated explicitly).**
- **Daylight from two sides** whenever possible, especially in larger rooms. [FP2] **Freq: several.**
- **Built-in wardrobes.** He hates the North American reach-in closet, "little rooms with doors that are pretending to be closets". *A 90 cm full-height built-in plus a dresser is enough in a small bedroom. A 90 × 90 cm square shower freed the space for it. It still fit a 180 cm mattress.* [DREAM, FP1] **Freq: several.**
- **Put wardrobes on the hallway wall** as an acoustic and mental buffer. [FP5] **Freq: once.**
- **A guest suite should not be "just a bed in a room".** Add an armchair in a view corner, and a stool if there's no room for a nightstand. [FP1] **Freq: once.**
- **Small windows over each nightstand** suit traditional homes. [FP8] **Freq: once.**
- **Plan for flexibility.** An office should be big enough to become a guest room or a full bedroom later. Rentable suites need their own entry into a mini-foyer, not straight into the bedroom. [FP1, FP3, FP8] **Freq: several.**

### 3.7 Primary suite

- **Enter through a small vestibule** that connects bedroom, bath and closet. It makes the suite private, removes sight lines from the bed to the door, lets one partner sleep while the other gets ready, and is "psychologically nice". [FP2, FP4, FP8] **Freq: constantly.**
- **Keep the closet in proportion to the bedroom.** An oversized U-shaped walk-in "rubs me the wrong way". [FP1] **Freq: several.**
- **Closets should be linear, like kitchens. No corners.** *60 cm deep hanging on one side, 30–40 cm shelves and drawers on the other; 2.5 linear m of hanging was enough.* Walk-ins are a good use of a dark interior corner, but he loves one with a window and counter-height drawers under it. [FP1, FP4, DREAM, FP2] **Freq: several.**
- **Don't open the walk-in directly onto the bed** when there is already circulation space to open it from. [FP1] **Freq: once.**
- **Closet accessed through the bathroom is controversial.** He has no strong view but always asks, because people worry about steam and about the closet being blocked while the bathroom is in use. [CONTRO] **Freq: once.**
- **Primary bedroom on the main level** for ageing in place. [DREAM] **Freq: once.**
- **Connect to the outdoors privately.** Doors from bedroom and bath onto a private terrace or deck section, screened with planting. Avoid a corner bath window you can see into from the social deck, or fix it with shades. [FP2, FP5, FP7, DREAM] **Freq: several.**

### 3.8 Bathrooms

- **Enter water closets and showers from the side, not the end.** End entry "really feels like a public stall". [FP5] **Freq: once (strongly stated).**
- **Don't let the WC door open onto a spa space.** A freestanding tub under a window is a spa space. Tuck the WC around a corner, and put it in the corner with no window. [FP4, FP2] **Freq: several.**
- **Double vanity on a run that doesn't straddle the shower and WC doors.** *Nearly 7 ft of counter.* He would extend the footprint by about 1 ft to get the proportions right. [FP2] **Freq: once.**
- **Wet room.** Put tub and shower together in one wet zone, ideally with a view. He prefers an undermount or built-in tub. Freestanding tubs need real clearance to clean, so in most bathrooms he sides with the "haters". [FP2, CONTRO, DREAM] **Freq: several.**
- **Minimum dimensions he cites:**
  - *18 in (45 cm) vanity counter plus 36 in (90 cm) clearance*
  - *A full bath with shower fits in 7 ft 6 in (2.3 m) of length*
  - *90 × 90 cm shower*

  [FP2, FP8, FP1] **Freq: once each.**
- **Shared kids' bath.** A separate WC is good so several children can use it at once, but a whole separate shower room wastes floor space. The sinks need a door between them and the entry. [FP5] **Freq: once.**
- **Bring daylight into the bathroom.** Add a window opposite the pocket door, or a big window lighting the shower. Handle privacy with sill height and light-filtering shades meant to stay closed, rather than losing the window. [FP1, FP6, FP8] **Freq: several.**
- **Avoid street-facing ground-floor bathrooms.** [FP8] **Freq: once.**
- **Plumbing walls shared with bedrooms are fine** if properly insulated and built. Don't let fear of noise limit the plan. [CONTRO] **Freq: once.**
- **Powder rooms and small windowless rooms are for fun:** a strong colour, one "star" element such as the sink, and supporting lighting. [KJ, TS] **Freq: several.**

### 3.9 Closets and storage

- **Depth matters:**
  - *60 cm*: coat closet or hanging
  - *30 cm*: bookcase or shelves (more comfortable, and doesn't jut into the space)
  - *18 cm*: open kitchen shelves

  [FP3, FP1, KIT] **Freq: several.**
- **Prefer built-ins to freestanding pieces.** Use full-height built-in walls (library or storage walls), not loose bookcases that leave dead gaps. A full library wall should hold "things we actually use", with no filler décor. [DREAM, SA1, SA2] **Freq: several.**
- **Clean sight lines matter more than one more closet.** He will remove a linen cupboard to fix a toilet view. [FP1] **Freq: once.**
- **Railings that double as storage partitions** (shelving or cabinets) appeal to him. [FP5] **Freq: once.**
- **No garage means extra storage elsewhere** for brooms, vacuums and supplies, usually a proper laundry room. [FP1] **Freq: once.**

### 3.10 Laundry and mudroom

- **A proper laundry room,** not a stacked washer-dryer closet, "in a house this size". It should hold a hamper, supply storage and a folding space. [FP1, FP5] **Freq: several.**
- **Put laundry on the bedroom level when you can.** He calls it "a luxury that I think is worth considering". Give it direct access from the bedroom hallway so you don't carry baskets through the kitchen and dining room. [FP8, FP7] **Freq: several.**
- **Chain the service rooms.** Pantry, laundry and mudroom flow into each other through cased openings or pocket doors, with doors only where laundry noise matters. A glass door keeps daylight. Give the mudroom windows, cubbies and a bench. [FP6, FP1] **Freq: several.**

### 3.11 Stairs

- **Don't put the stairs right in front of the entry.** Upstairs is the most private part of the house, so it is odd to pass through the foyer "on your way to make coffee". He bends or turns stairs so they run off a hallway instead, using the same number of risers. A front door looking straight up the stair into a bathroom is a red flag. [FP8] **Freq: several.**
- **If the stair is near the entry,** let the foyer see the bottom of the stair with a nice banister, not a basement door that "can read like a closet". [FP6] **Freq: once.**
- **Stairs deserve design attention.** He loves architecturally significant wood stairs, spiral stairs and good banisters. He is disappointed by builder-grade, drywall-wrapped stairs. "A staircase is so fun to design." [COOL, INSP1, DREAM] **Freq: several.**
- **Stack basement stairs under the main stair** to free floor space. [FP8, FP6] **Freq: several.**
- **Use stair details to enable alignment.** *He made a requested 4 ft stair 3 ft 11 in and moved one step so that three windows could centre on both the stair and the rear windows.* [FP4] **Freq: once.**
- **Put a window on the landing.** He split a stair at a landing in front of a window to divide the primary suite from the kids' wing. Light coming down a stair "will automatically draw people up". [FP6, FP5] **Freq: several.**

### 3.12 Windows and daylight

- **Place windows for the interior, then make them compose on the façade.** Centre windows on rooms, bays and axes. Align mullions to roof pitches and door lines. Windows that are "randomly punched" are a failure. "It might seem like I'm obsessed with aligning things, but it really does elevate a space." [FP1, FP3, FP4, FP5, INSP1] **Freq: constantly.**
- **Punctuate the ends of axes with windows.** A hallway, a pocket-door enfilade, the end of the kitchen run, or a circulation path should end in a window, not a blank wall. [FP1, FP3, FP5, FP6] **Freq: constantly.**
- **Use clerestories for light from the sun side and big glazing for the view side.** When sun and view point in opposite directions, he proposes split-shed or clerestory roofs. When there is a close neighbour, he uses high windows that show only sky over a full-height kitchen or library wall. [FP2, FP5] **Freq: several.**
- **Daylight from two sides** in large rooms. One solid wall (for example a bookcase) plus light from several sides feels comfortable rather than exposed. [FP2, INSP1] **Freq: several.**
- **Avoid a fishbowl.** Balance a big open back wall with high-sill windows on the other side for privacy and coziness. [FP8] **Freq: once.**
- **Use glazed interior partitions** (foyer to office, kitchen to dining) to share daylight and sight lines. [FP6, FP7, CONTRO] **Freq: several.**
- **Window treatments:**
  - Bare windows feel cold.
  - No roller or blackout blinds ("99% of the time the wrong choice").
  - Curtains hung near the ceiling, "kissing the floor", outside the frame.
  - Roman shades for high sills.
  - Café curtains for street privacy.
  - No films.

  [SA6, SA2, EC, APT] **Freq: constantly (in furnishing videos).**
- **Skylights and openings follow the structure,** for example a skylight between beams or exactly as wide as the bay below it. [EC] **Freq: several.**

### 3.13 Ceilings

- **Human scale.** He dislikes "soaring ceilings" in houses. He likes a vaulted living space with exposed structure, and lower ceilings in bedrooms. [DREAM, FP1] **Freq: several.**
- **Use ceiling height to define zones.** A clerestory band, beam or lowered soffit separates kitchen from living, or hallway from living room. [FP2, FP4] **Freq: several.**
- **In traditional houses, use cased openings,** because they give each room a ceiling corner. "Nothing looks stranger than just a singular flat ceiling spanning between a bunch of different rooms in a traditional home." Gutting walls in pre-war houses creates "builder-grade student housing energy". [FP8] **Freq: several ("say it with me").**
- **Do something interesting overhead:** hand-painted ceilings, cork ceilings, crown molding where it fits the architecture, planks run across a narrow room to widen it. [SV, EC, INSP1, SA4] **Freq: several.**

### 3.14 Exterior, massing, roofs and façade

- **The exterior should express the interior.** Bump-outs, stone cladding and special rooflines are only justified when they relate to an interior function. A leftover bump-out that holds only a closet is "completely useless and random", so delete it. [FP4, FP6] **Freq: several.**
- **Push and pull of masses.** A flat box "about as thoughtfully as a unit in a large apartment complex" is a failure. He carves porches, lowers roofs over single-storey bump-outs, and avoids putting living space above a bay so the mass stays "gentle". [FP1, FP6] **Freq: several.**
- **Make the garage its own lower mass.** Living space above a garage often makes the roof "wonky", pushing it into "McMansion territory". [FP6] **Freq: once.**
- **Tie roof pitch to the plan.** Move the ridge to align with a new wall so it "feels structurally cohesive". Align the fridge or tall storage with the vault pitch inside. [FP1] **Freq: once.**
- **Hierarchy of doors on the façade.** The main entry should read as primary. Clad or paint a secondary (rental) door like the siding, and give the main porch a contrasting material. [FP1, FP7] **Freq: several.**
- **No performative façades.** He dislikes houses with all the interest on the street face and flat boxes on the other three sides, a ceremonial front door the family never uses, and formal front rooms. "About the people out there, not the people in here." [SUBURB] **Freq: several.**
- **No chaotic or misaligned window façades.** This is one of his "five worst" things architects do. "I passionately disapprove." [WORST] **Freq: several.**
- **No fake architectural detail:** faux dormers ("tisk tisk"), foam columns, stick-on mullions. Fake detail fails both function and authenticity. [WORST, MOD, DIY] **Freq: constantly.**
- **No open glass or baluster railings on city balconies.** You feel exposed, "people can see your ankles". Use solid railings in urban settings and open ones only for rural views. Use low, sturdy furniture to feel anchored. [WORST] **Freq: once.**
- **Symmetry should not damage the interior.** He follows a symmetric farmhouse language where it suits the house, but criticizes symmetry imposed at the expense of the plan. [FP6, SV] **Freq: several.**
- **Mid-century and Eichler-style privacy to the street** (courtyards, breeze-block walls, closed fronts) is a better answer to daylight and privacy than showy façades. [SUBURB] **Freq: once.**
- **Use local materials.** He praises locally sourced stone and brick scaled to the hand. New additions should defer to old buildings: lower, glazed links, a courtyard between. [INSP3, INSP1] **Freq: several.**

### 3.15 Furniture layout (applied to plan reviews)

- **Float social furniture off the walls** in central clusters. Storage pieces can go on walls. [SPACE, SA5] **Freq: constantly.**
- **Rugs must suit the scale of the room:**
  - *Living rugs about 2.5 × 3.5 m to 3 × 4 m*
  - *150 × 200 cm is "timid"*
  - At minimum, the front legs of all seating sit on the rug.
  - The long edge runs parallel to the main sofa.

  [SA6, CB, SA5, SA3] **Freq: constantly.**
- **No L-shaped sectional or chaise in a social living room.** Put it in the den. [SA2, SA6, SA5, SA3] **Freq: constantly.**
- **Never put boxy furniture across a corner at 45°.** "A strict rule." [SPACE] **Freq: several.**
- **TV viewing distance about 2× the TV width.** Big rooms with furniture spread too far apart feel "like a hotel lobby". [SPACE] **Freq: once.**
- **Two armchairs with a gap make a softer zone boundary** than a sofa back, which is "a harsh partition". [SA4] **Freq: several.**
- **Keep as much floor visible as possible in small spaces.** Avoid bulky pieces. [SA1] **Freq: several.**
- **Walls:** art should be about ⅔ the width of the furniture under it; hang it low so it relates to the furniture; leave blank "resting" walls between zones. [SA6, SA3, SA2] **Freq: several.**

### 3.16 Lighting (relevant at plan stage)

- **Avoid even grids of recessed cans.** They are "thoughtless". Light the surfaces and perimeter walls, use one dominant fixture per open space, and use lots of lamps at night. "The big light" is for daytime only. [LIGHT, SA3] **Freq: constantly.**
- **About 3000K.** *Below about 2000K is too orange; above 4000K is cold.* Read the Kelvin number on the spec, not the label. [LIGHT] **Freq: once (detailed).**
- **No overused LED strip lighting.** One of the "five worst": it feels "commercial and flashy". One example he gives is a bar lit down onto people's legs, designed for the photo and not for lived use. [WORST, LIGHT] **Freq: several.**
- **No exposed or flame-shaped bulbs, cage pendants, or "boob lights".** A fixture's job is to shape light. [LIGHT, SV, KJ] **Freq: constantly.**
- **Plan sconces and lamp positions** while doing the space plan, with outlets and wall-mounted arms. [APT, COOL] **Freq: several.**

### 3.17 Materials, colour and ornament (for spec reviews)

- **Authentic materials only.** No wood-look vinyl, printed tile, faux marble, plastic plants or artificial turf walls. He would rather use an honest cheap material (honest vinyl, laminate, engineered wood, veneer) than an imitation of an expensive one. "Good design doesn't need to be expensive but must be honest." [FAUX, MAT, BAD2, WORST] **Freq: constantly.**
- **Choose materials in this order:** function and constraints first (wet, cleanable), then budget, then context. If you love marble but can't afford it, ask what you love about it and find an honest material with that quality. [FAUX] **Freq: several.**
- **Contrast across spectra.** Hard to soft, light to dark value, rough to smooth texture. Vary wood tones in value but keep the same undertone. [MAT, SA4, CB] **Freq: constantly.**
- **Colour should highlight architecture,** not be arbitrary accent walls. Use low chroma in everyday rooms; draw palettes from nature and local materials. [COLOR, MOD] **Freq: several.**
- **"Dated" is not a flaw if a material is authentic.** Honey oak and pink tile are fine; fake is never timeless. [IHATE, MAT] **Freq: several.**
- **Context over style.** Regional styles respond to climate, so don't transplant them. Don't strip or paint original woodwork. Preserve the existing character. [STYLES, CB, FP8] **Freq: constantly.**

### 3.18 Cost and efficiency

- **Find the cheap structural move.** He would rather move a bedroom wall than a kitchen ("moving a kitchen is pretty serious business and pretty expensive"). In the condo he kept the kitchen and moved the bedroom so the existing window lit the whole living space. [FP3] **Freq: once (but a clear principle).**
- **Small edits, big impact.** He shifts walls by inches, takes "a few inches from each room" to gain a foot in the living area, and grows the footprint only by about 1 ft where proportions demand it. He keeps roughly the same square footage overall. [FP1, FP2, FP6, FP5] **Freq: constantly.**
- **Most constraints in a new build are self-imposed**, so question everything early. Late in design, work inside the roof line and footprint, for example carving a porch out of the existing envelope. [FP2, FP1] **Freq: several.**
- **Spend on the two or three architectural moments that matter,** for example special doors flanking the fireplace and the clerestory band, not on everything. [FP2] **Freq: once.**
- **Hire an interior designer early,** before the architect's plan is fixed. "It's never too soon to start prioritizing the interior." [FP1] **Freq: several (end-of-video plug).**

### 3.19 Outdoor spaces

- **Courtyards are his favourite feature.** They bring daylight and nature into the centre of the house. Plant large plants and trees in the ground, not just pots on concrete. He shrank a 6 × 3 m pool to a 3 × 3 m plunge pool to make room for planting and seating. He removed a pointless courtyard fence. [FP7, DREAM, SUBURB] **Freq: constantly.**
- **Blur inside and outside:** deep eaves, big doors that open fully, built-in seating at the openings, a living room opening onto the deck. [DREAM, FP8, FP2] **Freq: constantly.**
- **Align hardscape with the interior grid.** Extend interior zone lines outside and let house corners act as edges for outdoor rooms. [FP3] **Freq: several.**
- **Deck and terrace layout:**
  - Centre seating groups on the doors.
  - Put low seating, not dining, on the view side so it blocks less of the view.
  - Leave plenty of empty terrace so it feels expansive.
  - Give each outdoor zone a function (barbecue, dining, lounge).

  [FP2, FP5] **Freq: several.**
- **Hardscape:** brick, stone, tile or gravel rather than asphalt or poured concrete, even for a driveway ("suffocating"). Landscape should be manicured near the house and wilder further out. [DREAM, INSP3] **Freq: several.**
- **No artificial grass,** least of all as "living walls" ("dystopian"). [WORST] **Freq: several.**

## 4. Pet peeves and red flags he calls out repeatedly

When role-playing, scan for these first, roughly in order of how often he raises them:

1. **A toilet visible** from the kitchen, living area, bed, front door, top of the stairs or the bathroom doorway. His fix: "flip the bathroom".
2. **Blunt entry.** Front door straight into the living room, no porch, vestibule or drop zone, two competing front doors.
3. **Circulation eating the rooms.** "Path, not room". Paths through all four corners of the living room. Every space feeling like a hallway.
4. **Paths through the kitchen work zone,** or squeezing between stools and dining chairs.
5. **Kitchen corners, L/U kitchens, and 45° anything.**
6. **Misalignment.** Windows not centred on rooms or axes, islands not aligned to walls, random openings on the façade.
7. **Leftover or performative features.** Bump-outs, cladding or roofline flourishes that no longer relate to the interior. Faux dormers. A façade-only design.
8. **Dead or badly distributed space.** An empty quadrant, an oversized primary bedroom or closet, a bedroom more than twice the bed's length, an oversized powder room.
9. **TV hung high over the fireplace.** Corner fireplaces.
10. **Formal dining or living rooms** nobody uses.
11. **Stacked laundry closet** in a house big enough for a laundry room. Laundry far from the bedrooms.
12. **Stairs dumping into the foyer,** or a front door looking straight up the stairs.
13. **Wrong-way door swings.** Doors jammed into corners. Too many doors opening onto one small room.
14. **WC or shower entered from the end** ("public stall"). A WC door opening onto a spa tub. A freestanding tub without cleaning clearance.
15. **Reach-in closets** pretending to be rooms. U-shaped walk-ins with dead corners.
16. **Open glass railings on city balconies.** Overused LED strips. Grids of can lights. Exposed or flame bulbs.
17. **Faux anything:** materials, plants, turf, mullions, painted-on wood grain.
18. **L-shaped sectionals** in social rooms. Furniture at 45°. Undersized rugs.
19. **Gutted traditional houses** with one flat ceiling across what used to be several rooms.
20. **Indoor and outdoor dining tables back to back** across glass.

## 6. Where he is US-centric or climate- and culture-specific

His defaults come from cool and temperate climates in North America and Northern Europe, plus Australia. He does acknowledge culture and climate in a few places: entry routines, open or closed kitchens, island seating, and the note that Los Angeles doesn't need heavy curtains. The list below covers what to adapt for a hot, dry site in Guntur, Andhra Pradesh. Those adaptation notes are my **inference** unless stated otherwise.

| His default | Why it's local to him | How it transfers to a hot-dry Indian site (inference) |
|---|---|---|
| **Capture sun from the equator side** (south in the US, north in Australia) with large windows or clerestories | Cold climates want solar gain | **Reverse the instinct.** Keep the *principle* (clerestory light from one side, view from the other, daylight from two sides) but block direct sun. Use deep eaves and verandahs, high north-facing clerestories, and limited west glazing. He does love deep eaves, and that rule transfers well. |
| **Big glazed walls and doors** onto decks and terraces | Temperate climates; he notes "in moderate/temperate climates people love to occupy edges" | The indoor-outdoor *edge* transfers well (verandah, *thinnai*, courtyard), but it needs to be shaded, and glass areas need to be smaller or screened. |
| **Courtyard love** | Modernist and Eichler references | **Transfers very well.** It matches the Kerala *nadumuttam* (central courtyard). His rules on planting in the ground and keeping hallways along the courtyard all apply. |
| **Cozy, thick textiles, rugs, heavy curtains, fireplaces, wood stoves** | Copenhagen winters. He himself says LA "doesn't need insulation". | Mostly **doesn't transfer**. Swap in light cotton or linen, operable shutters and jaalis. His ban on blackout or roller blinds may not hold where glare and heat control matter. |
| **Mudroom, coat closet, garage entry, basement stairs** | Snow, mud and car-based suburbs | Shoes-off is common in India and matches his genkan idea well. Keep a shoe and drop zone at the entry. Coat storage and a heavy mudroom aren't needed, though monsoon wet gear might justify a small one. |
| **Open or semi-open kitchen; kitchen "takes a back seat"; island as furniture** | Western cooking with less smoke and smell | He himself calls open versus closed "highly cultural". Indian tadka and frying favour a **closed or glass-partitioned kitchen plus a work or utility area**, which fits his "glass partition" option. Island clearance and no-corner rules still apply. |
| **Dishwasher placement, integrated fridge, freestanding tubs, wet rooms with tub** | Western fixtures | Dishwashers and tubs are optional. His sink, prep and landing-counter logic transfers. |
| **No formal dining room; multi-use central table** | A reaction to the North American formal dining room | Transfers well for everyday living. Indian hosting (large family gatherings, festivals) supports his "big table that expands" idea. |
| **Separate den ("away room") for TV** | Open-plan Western houses | Transfers: a TV or family room apart from the formal or guest living area is common in Indian homes. |
| **Reach-in closets bad, built-in wardrobes good** | A North American habit | Built-in wardrobes are already the Indian norm. Transfers. |
| **Laundry room on the bedroom level** | Dryer culture ("very important as an American") | In India, washing is often done in a utility area with outdoor or terrace drying, so plan the laundry near the drying space rather than the bedrooms. |
| **Formal living room / McMansion / HOA / curb-appeal critique** | US suburbia | His principle "house designed for the people inside" transfers. The specific targets don't. |
| **Imperial units with metric** | American who lives in Europe | Use metric. |
| **Walls: drywall, cased openings, crown molding** | US timber-frame and drywall construction | In AAC or masonry, a "cased opening" is a framed or plastered opening with a lintel. His point about ceiling corners between rooms still applies. |
| **Toilet sight lines, door swings, circulation, alignment, linear kitchens, vestibules, proportions** | Universal human-comfort rules | **Fully transferable.** In India, *vastu* will sometimes fix toilet and kitchen positions, so expect to work his rules around those. |
| **Shoes-off, genkan-style sunken entry** | His personal preference | Fully transferable, and a natural fit. |

He has never reviewed an Indian or hot-dry plan in these videos. The tropical Mexico villa [FP1] is the closest, and there he focused only on circulation and sight lines, not climate. So anything he "would say" about monsoon, vastu or heat is our extrapolation.
